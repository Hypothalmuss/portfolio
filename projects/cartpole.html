Project 04 — Reinforcement Learning

CartPole Goes Brrr

One cartpole rig, nine increasingly ridiculous jobs — from the textbook balance problem to a stick figure walking from human motion capture. Learning RL properly, which mostly meant learning how many ways a reward function can lie to you.

The same rig, reconfigured across tasks.

The problem

Take one physics rig and give it progressively harder jobs it was never designed for, so that each failure isolates something specific about reward design. The real subject is not the policies — it is that a reward function always finds the cheapest thing that satisfies it, and "cheapest" is almost never what you meant.

The challenges

A success rate that was never real

The two-link balance task reported 30% success for months. It was actually zero. The termination check tested an angle the pole physically could not reach — link1's collision geometry caught it on the floor first — so a face-plant ran out the clock and scored as a win. Underneath sat three more bugs: penalties linear in raw Newtons that made using the actuator net-negative (the agent settled on 6.6 N of a 100 N budget), an un-normalised action space, and 240 Hz control leaving gamma=0.99 just 0.42 s of lookahead.

Rebuilt with geometric tip-height termination, a normalised action space, 60 Hz control and nine reward terms collapsed into one, it runs at a genuine 63% — and 36 of the 37 failures are the cart running out of rail with the pole still upright.

"Moved forward" is not "walked"

The planar biped reached 6.4 m per episode against a random baseline of −0.02 m. It was skating: both feet on the ground 98% of the time, knees never leaving their straight hard stop, more foot slip than forward travel. Forward velocity paid identically whether the robot stepped or slid, and nothing in the reward objected — the same shape of hole as the unreachable termination check, a metric measuring something adjacent to the thing wanted.

PPO destroying a policy it had already learned

The imitation run reached near-full-episode tracking at 1.1M steps, then collapsed. approx_kl hit 100× its healthy range as the policy sharpened, and ten unguarded epochs per batch kept overshooting. The final model evaluated at 0% while its own 1.1M checkpoint evaluated at 80%. Run 2 added target_kl, learning-rate decay, and dropped reward normalisation — 99%, no collapse.

The fix: demonstrate instead of specify

The walking reward lineage — slip penalties, clearance bonuses, step machines, alternation discounts, overtake rules — was seven terms of hand-crafted economics, and it bought skating, then shuffling, then a gallop. The final task throws all of it out for one idea from DeepMimic: a reference motion says where every joint should be at every phase, and the reward is closeness to it.

Three mechanisms carry the training, all load-bearing: gait phase in the observation, reference-state initialisation, and early termination when the pose diverges.

CMU's capture, the human skeleton, the retargeted reference, and the policy in real physics — phase-locked.

Outcome

Every real gain here came from finding a broken measurement, not a better hyperparameter. One geometric quantity beat nine hand-weighted terms; one reference trajectory bought clearance, alternation and no-slip for free; and PPO proved it can destroy a working policy as efficiently as it builds one when nothing bounds the update size.

Most RL write-ups show the run that worked. This repository keeps the post-mortems — the 0% reported as 30%, the skating reported as walking, the training collapse — because the ability to distrust your own results is the part that transfers to real robots. On hardware, a metric that lies costs more than a policy that underperforms.
