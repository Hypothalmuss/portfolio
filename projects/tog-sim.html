Project 02 — Industrial Manipulation

tog-sim — Vision-Guided Pick & Place

A full packaging cell: a neural network segments unsorted products on a moving belt, a SCARA picks them with a vacuum gripper, and places them into trays that are also moving — the belts never stop. Built as an open re-interpretation of a Schubert tog.519.

Why this is hard

The naive version of pick and place is easy: stop the belt, look, pick, place, restart. Every second of stopped belt is lost throughput, so real packaging machines never do that. They pick from a belt that is still moving, into a tray that is also still moving, which means the robot is aiming at two independently moving targets and has to arrive at a point neither of them has reached yet.

On top of that the products arrive unsorted and unoriented. There is no fixture telling the robot where anything is — perception has to supply the grasp pose, the product class, and which tray pockets are still empty.

The cell: SCARA with tilt module and suction cup between the infeed belt (products) and outfeed belt (trays), two overhead D435s, Open-RMF enclosure — all in Gazebo Fortress.

Cartons only at 60 products/min on the fast motion profile, vision perception live from Gazebo — continuous pick and place from moving belts, no stops.

Training a network with no real data

There is no dataset of Schubert packaging cells, so the perception model was trained entirely on data the simulator generated: 1,773 synthetic scenes, a third of them containing bar-style trays, rendered with panoptic segmentation cameras that hand back pixel-perfect labels for free. No manual annotation at any point.

The resulting YOLO11n-seg model reaches 0.98 mask mAP50 — and more usefully, when its outputs are compared to ground truth on the metrics that actually matter downstream (is the class right, is the grasp height right, is the yaw right), it is accurate to well under a millimetre.

Engineering note — the one-line bug that eats a week

Ultralytics treats NumPy images as BGR. ROS publishes rgb8. Feed a ROS frame straight into inference and the model sees every product in the wrong colour space — it still detects things, just badly and inconsistently, which is far harder to diagnose than an outright failure. The nodes now convert explicitly before inference, and the training PNGs are written as BGR to match.

Picking off a moving belt

The conveyor_tracker node turns per-frame detections into stable tracks for both products and trays, and publishes each as a TF frame. That single design choice is what makes the rest tractable: once a moving product is a coordinate frame, the motion planner can aim at where it will be rather than where it was seen, and the pick becomes a normal transform lookup instead of a special case.

On top of that sits a TRACK_CART motion mode that flies, tracks and seals against a target in motion. Each cycle runs as four phases — schedule (what is pickable now and where can it go), pick (which also measures the grasp offset it actually achieved), choose_pocket, and place — so the tray decision is revisited after the pick with knowledge of how the product actually sits in the gripper.

Teal blocks are project code; the SCARA controller and simulator are the surrounding infrastructure.

Mixed run — cartons and bars at 30 products/min across two tray models on the outfeed. Every product class routes to a tray that fits it; 8/8 cycles at 4.4–4.5 mm mean, no tray disturbed.

Benchmarking like it matters

Robotics demos are easy to cherry-pick, so the numbers here come from a harness rather than a good run. bench.sh executes seeded repeats and writes one JSON per run — cycles, attempts, picks per minute, motion time, placement mean and p95 against ground truth, a per-phase timeline, and failures. bench_report.py then aggregates them with 95% confidence intervals: Wilson intervals for success rate, bootstrap over cycles for picks-per-minute, bootstrap over placed products for placement error.

The table regenerates from data; only the interpretation is written by hand. Tuning parameters live in versioned YAML alongside the rationale for each — including the levers that were tried and rejected, which is usually the more useful half.

Operator-facing, not just demo-facing

M5 added togsim_hmi: a FastAPI + vanilla-JS panel with status, belt control, recipes, start/stop/e-stop, alarms with explicit acknowledgement, health indicators, run history and live tray occupancy. It exists because a cell that only a developer can start is not a cell — the question "what is it doing and how do I stop it" has to have an answer that isn't a terminal.

A detail I'm quietly proud of

tog-sim runs on its own ROS_DOMAIN_ID (42 by default). Another Gazebo instance on the same machine or LAN publishes /clock too, and if it mixes with yours you get a simulation whose time jumps — a bug that presents as inexplicable motion glitches and takes days to trace. Isolating the domain by default makes that class of failure impossible rather than rare.
