Project 02 — Industrial Manipulation

tog-sim — Vision-Guided Pick & Place

A packaging cell where a neural network segments unsorted products on a moving belt, a SCARA picks them with a vacuum gripper, and places them into trays that are also moving — the belts never stop.

The problem

The naive version is easy: stop the belt, look, pick, place, restart. Every second of stopped belt is lost throughput, so real packaging machines never do that. They pick from a belt that is still moving into a tray that is also still moving — two independently moving targets, and the robot must arrive at a point neither has reached yet.

The products arrive unsorted and unoriented, with no fixture telling the robot where anything is. Perception has to supply the grasp pose, the product class, and which tray pockets are still free.

Cartons and bars at 30 products/min across two tray models — every product class routes to a tray that fits it, picked and placed from moving belts with no stops.

Architecture

Teal blocks are project code; the SCARA controller and simulator surround them.

The challenges

Aiming at a target that is still moving

The conveyor_tracker node turns per-frame detections into stable tracks for products and trays, and publishes each as a TF frame. That one choice makes the rest tractable: once a moving product is a coordinate frame, the planner can aim at where it will be, and the pick becomes a transform lookup rather than a special case. A TRACK_CART motion mode flies, tracks and seals against the target in motion.

Training a network with no real data

No dataset of packaging cells exists, so perception trained entirely on 1,773 synthetic scenes rendered with panoptic segmentation cameras that return pixel-perfect labels for free — no manual annotation anywhere in the pipeline.

The one-line bug that eats a week

Ultralytics treats NumPy images as BGR; ROS publishes rgb8. Feed a ROS frame straight into inference and the model sees every product in the wrong colour space — it still detects things, just badly and inconsistently, which is far harder to diagnose than an outright failure.

Measuring honestly

Robotics demos are easy to cherry-pick, so the numbers come from a harness rather than a good run. Seeded repeats write one JSON per run; aggregation applies 95% confidence intervals — Wilson for success rate, bootstrap over cycles for picks-per-minute and over products for placement error. Tuning parameters live in versioned YAML alongside the rationale for each, including the levers tried and rejected.

Perception validated against simulator ground truth rather than itself: class accuracy 1.00, grasp height within 0.4 mm, yaw within 0.8°, tray pose within 4 mm / 0.05°. Pocket clearance is 5 mm per side, so a 2.7 mm mean is the difference between a product seating and one jamming.

Outcome

A complete cell rather than a demo: perception, motion, task scheduling and an operator HMI with recipes, alarms, e-stop and run history — because a cell only a developer can start is not a cell. The throughput and accuracy numbers hold up under a statistical harness rather than on a lucky take, and the whole thing runs on its own ROS_DOMAIN_ID so a second Gazebo on the same network can never contaminate its clock.
