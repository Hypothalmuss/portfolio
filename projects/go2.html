Project 01 — Legged Autonomy

Go2 Quadruped — Autonomous Exploration

A Unitree Go2 walks into a building it has never seen, chooses its own goals, maps the space, and stops when there is nothing left to explore. No operator, no prior map, no waypoints.

Hardware is off-the-shelf. The autonomy stack on top of it is the work.

The problem

Survey work: send a quadruped into a space and get back a map, plus the confidence that it never walked anywhere it shouldn't have. That rules out teleoperation, and it rules out pre-planned waypoint routes — you cannot plan waypoints through a building you have not mapped yet. The robot has to choose its own goals from what it has seen so far.

Exploration run at 4× — Gazebo left, RViz showing the occupancy grid filling in as the robot picks its own frontier goals.

Architecture

A loop, not a pipeline. The mission manager sits above everything, deciding whether the current goal is still worth pursuing, and the geofence vetoes anything leaving the permitted polygon regardless of what the planner wants.

Teal blocks are the parts I wrote; the rest is ROS 2 infrastructure wired to them.

The challenges

Goals the robot can see but cannot reach

A frontier just past a doorway too narrow for the robot, or one behind a wall it can perceive over. The planner accepts the goal, the robot drives at it, gets close, then grinds — nudging, re-planning, nudging — forever. The mission never advances and the map never completes.

The original recovery only triggered while the obstacle-avoidance layer had paused the robot. That covered the obvious case and missed the common one: stuck against a wall during a final orientation spin, where avoidance never fires because nothing is in front. Those control loops had no exit condition.

The fix made the timeout a property of the motion rather than of the avoidance state. Every waypoint-following and orientation loop enforces its own deadline, set to a multiple of expected travel time. On expiry the goal is marked unreachable, the mission manager discards it, and the explorer picks a fresh frontier — a few seconds lost instead of the whole run.

Extending an indoor stack outdoors

Larger open spaces put frontiers further apart, so a bad choice costs more. Uneven ground starts to break the flat-world assumption behind a 2D occupancy grid, which is why the OctoMap layer matters — overhangs and low obstacles stay visible to the planner instead of flattening away. GPS arrives as both a global reference and the geofence that makes outdoor autonomy permissible.

Results

  • Full autonomous coverage of indoor spaces with no operator input and no prior map — the run terminates on its own when frontiers are exhausted.
  • Unreachable goals degraded from a mission-ending stall to a few seconds of lost time.
  • Stack extended from indoor-only to outdoor sites, with geofencing enforced independently of the planner.
  • Reactive avoidance handles moving obstacles rather than assuming a static map.

Reactive avoidance against a moving obstacle crossing the robot's path.

Outcome

The SLAM and the planner were the solved parts — good open-source implementations exist for both. The engineering that mattered was everything deciding what to do when those solved problems return an answer that doesn't work: unreachable goals, frontiers that aren't really frontiers, a robot technically making progress but practically stuck. A demo runs for two minutes; an autonomous survey has to run for an hour unattended, and the gap between those two is entirely a question of how many failure modes you have handled.
