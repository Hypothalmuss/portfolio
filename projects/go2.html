Project 01 — Legged Autonomy

Go2 Quadruped — Autonomous Exploration

A Unitree Go2 walks into a building it has never seen, decides where to go on its own, maps the whole thing, and comes back — no operator, no pre-supplied map, no waypoints typed in by a human.

Hardware is off-the-shelf; the autonomy stack on top of it is the work.

The problem

A quadruped is only useful if someone doesn't have to drive it. The brief was survey work: send the robot into a space — a floor of a building, a site perimeter — and get back a map plus the confidence that it never walked anywhere it shouldn't have.

That rules out teleoperation, and it rules out pre-planned waypoint routes, because you cannot plan waypoints through a building you have not mapped yet. The robot has to choose its own goals from what it has seen so far.

What I built

  • Frontier-based exploration. The robot continuously finds the boundaries between mapped free space and the unknown, ranks those frontiers, and drives to the one that buys the most new information for the distance travelled. When frontiers run out, the space is covered and the mission ends on its own.
  • SLAM and mapping pipelines in ROS 2. A 2D occupancy grid for planning, plus an OctoMap volumetric layer so overhangs, tables and low obstacles exist to the planner rather than vanishing into a flat projection — which matters a lot for a legged robot that can step over some things and must go around others.
  • Navigation and reactive obstacle avoidance. Nav2 for global planning, with a path-following layer that handles the gap between a planner's idea of a path and how a walking robot actually tracks it.
  • GPS geofencing. A hard, independently-enforced boundary. The robot can plan whatever it likes; if a goal or a trajectory leaves the permitted polygon, it is refused. On an outdoor site this is the difference between a survey tool and a liability.

Autonomous exploration run, played back at 4× — Gazebo on the left, RViz showing the occupancy grid filling in as the robot picks its own frontier goals, and the ROS 2 planner lifecycle in the terminal.

The hard part nobody warns you about

Frontier exploration has a failure mode that only shows up on real hardware: a goal the robot can see but cannot reach. A frontier just past a doorway the robot doesn't fit through, or one behind a wall it can perceive over. The planner accepts the goal, the robot drives at it, gets close, and then grinds — nudging, re-planning, nudging again, forever. The mission never advances and the map never completes.

Engineering note — the bug that mattered

The original recovery logic only triggered while the obstacle-avoidance layer had actively paused the robot. That covered the obvious case and missed the common one: a robot stuck against a wall during a final orientation spin, where avoidance never fires because nothing is in front of it. The control loops had no exit condition and would spin indefinitely.

The fix was to make the timeout a property of the motion itself rather than of the avoidance state — every waypoint-following and orientation loop enforces its own deadline, set to a multiple of the expected travel time. When it expires, the goal is marked unreachable, the mission manager discards it, and the explorer immediately picks a fresh frontier. One stuck goal costs a few seconds now instead of ending the mission.

Reactive obstacle avoidance against a moving obstacle — the robot re-plans around a body crossing its path rather than treating the map as static.

Indoors first, then outdoors

The system was built for interiors, where LiDAR SLAM is well-behaved and there is no GPS. Extending it outdoors changed the problem: larger and more open spaces, so frontiers are further apart and the cost of choosing a bad one is higher; uneven ground, where the flat-world assumption behind a 2D occupancy grid starts to lie; and the arrival of GPS, which brings both a global reference frame and the geofencing that makes outdoor autonomy permissible at all.

What I'd tell you in an interview

The interesting engineering here was not the SLAM or the planner — those are solved problems with good open-source implementations. It was everything that decides what to do when the solved problems return an answer that doesn't work: unreachable goals, frontiers that aren't really frontiers, a robot that is technically making progress but practically stuck. A demo runs for two minutes. An autonomous survey has to run for an hour without a human, and that is entirely a question of how many failure modes you have thought about.
