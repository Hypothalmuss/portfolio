Project 03 — Manipulation & Safety

UR3e — Pick-and-Place Cell

A UR3e with a Robotiq gripper, an overhead camera and a web dashboard. It finds cubes, works out which it can actually reach, refuses the ones it can't, and sorts the rest into a drop zone.

The problem

Build a work cell that a non-developer can drive: it should see what is on the table, decide what is actually achievable, do the achievable part, and stop safely on demand. The interesting constraints are not in the picking — they are in refusing work it cannot do, and stopping when told.

Multi-cube sweep — Gazebo left, the operator dashboard right showing live state, detected cubes with reach classification, joint positions and the activity log.

Architecture

Teal blocks are project code. Note the e-stop path: it goes straight to the controller rather than through the orchestrator, which is the whole point.

The challenges

Cancelling a goal does not stop an arm

The bug worth the whole project

Cancelling in-flight action goals is necessary but not sufficient. A trajectory already handed to the controller keeps executing regardless of what happens to the goal that produced it — the arm carries on for several radians while your "stop" successfully cancels nothing that matters.

The stop also has to command the controller to hold its current position. That is what actually brings the arm to rest. Measured drift after a stop: 0.0001 rad.

It then latches — every command except reset is refused until cleared. Deliberately distinct from stop, which is a tidy recovery that reopens the gripper and drives home. Tidying up is the wrong response to an emergency: if someone hit that button, you do not want the arm travelling back through whatever caused it.

IDs that survive occlusion

Each cube gets an ID that persists while the arm passes overhead. That sounds like a detail until the orchestrator has committed to "cube 4" and cannot have it renamed mid-cycle because a shadow briefly split one blob into two. Cubes not currently being picked are published to MoveIt as collision objects, so the arm plans around them rather than through them.

Testing a vision system without asking the vision system

Every physical claim is verified against the simulator's own model state, never against the perception pipeline — because that pipeline is one of the things under test, and a vision system asked to confirm its own output will always agree with itself. That includes checking placed cubes land in different slots, since four cubes stacked in one corner passes a naive "is it in the zone" test perfectly.

Results

  • Emergency stop brings the arm to rest with 0.0001 rad of drift, and latches until explicitly reset.
  • Cube identity survives occlusion by the arm across a full multi-cube cycle.
  • Unreachable cubes are refused on sight rather than attempted and failed.
  • ~30 system tests covering startup, input validation, every task, e-stop and reset — all checked against ground truth.
  • Operator dashboard runs from a bare Humble install with zero extra dependencies.

Outcome

A cell that behaves like equipment rather than a script: it declines impossible work, it stops when told and stays stopped, and it can be driven by someone who has never opened a terminal. Worth stating plainly — Gazebo Classic's contact solver will not hold a light box in a parallel gripper, so the grasp is physically real but the hold is plugin-assisted. A portfolio that hides its simulator's limits is telling you something about how it reports results.
