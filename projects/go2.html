Project 01 — Legged Autonomy

Unitree Quadruped Fleet — Autonomy & Simulation

The ROS 2 autonomy stack for a client fleet of about 45 Unitree quadrupeds. The robot explores space it has never seen, reacts to obstacles the map didn't have, and runs planned missions — and every one of those behaviours is proven in simulation before it reaches a robot.

Hardware is off-the-shelf. The autonomy stack on top of it is the work.

The problem

Clients send a quadruped into a site and expect it to come back with a map, or to run a mission they planned from a desk. That rules out teleoperation, and for exploration it rules out pre-planned routes too — you cannot plan waypoints through a building you have not mapped yet. The robot has to choose its own goals from what it has seen so far.

The second constraint is distance. The robots are deployed by the Italian team, across five Unitree models, so nothing can be debugged by walking over to the robot. The code has to be right before it ships, which makes the simulator part of the product rather than a convenience.

Two features, two robots, one stack: exploration, avoidance and mission planning all run on Go2, B2 and B2W. A2 and A2W support is in progress.

Architecture

A loop, not a pipeline. Exploration and mission planning both produce goals for the same A* planner; reactive avoidance runs beneath it and feeds what it sees straight back into the plan. Everything talks to the robot through the Unitree topic interface — which is exactly what the simulator bridge reproduces.

Teal blocks are the parts I wrote; the rest is ROS 2 infrastructure wired to them.

The challenges

Choosing where to go next

Exploration is a ROS 2 service: ask it for a goal, get the best frontier back. Frontier cells are found with an 8-neighbour test on the occupancy grid — free cells touching unknown space — then grouped with KD-tree clustering so one ragged doorway edge becomes one candidate rather than forty. Each cluster is scored by its medoid, trading distance against size: a large frontier nearby beats a sliver across the map. The medoid is always a real frontier cell, so the goal never lands inside a wall the way a centroid of a curved frontier can.

Goals the robot can see but cannot reach

A frontier just past a doorway too narrow for the robot, or one behind a wall it can perceive over. The planner accepts the goal, the robot drives at it, gets close, then grinds — nudging, re-planning, nudging — forever. The mission never advances and the map never completes.

The fix made the timeout a property of the motion rather than of the avoidance state. Every waypoint-following and orientation loop enforces its own deadline, set to a multiple of expected travel time. On expiry the goal is marked unreachable, discarded, and the explorer picks a fresh frontier — a few seconds lost instead of the whole run.

Obstacles the map didn't have

Reactive avoidance runs as ROS 2 actions on raw LiDAR, with two strategies: rotate, then wall-follow for something blocking the path, and a lateral slide for something that can be side-stepped. Dodging alone isn't enough — the global plan would just walk the robot back into the same obstacle — so detected obstacles are injected live into the A* planner, which replans locally around them.

Reactive avoidance against a moving obstacle crossing the robot's path.

A simulator the production stack can't tell apart

The Gazebo simulator covers Go2, A2, A2W, B2 and B2W. The important part is the bridge layer: it mirrors the real Unitree topic interface, so the production stack runs unchanged — no sim-only branches, no flags to forget before deployment. That is what makes it useful to clients, who validate their missions in it before anything runs on hardware.

Results

  • Autonomy code in production on a client fleet of about 45 Unitree quadrupeds.
  • A 7-minute exploration run covered 58 m across 8 frontier goals in simulation, with no operator input and no prior map.
  • Exploration, avoidance and mission planning supported on Go2, B2 and B2W; A2 and A2W support in progress.
  • Unreachable goals degraded from a mission-ending stall to a few seconds of lost time.
  • Clients validate missions in the simulator before hardware, against the same interface the robots expose.

Outcome

Mapping and path planning were the solved parts — good implementations exist for both. The engineering that mattered was everything deciding what to do when those solved problems return an answer that doesn't work: unreachable goals, frontiers that aren't really frontiers, an obstacle the map never had. And because the robots are deployed far from where the code is written, the simulator had to be faithful enough that passing in sim actually means something on hardware.
