Project 04 — Reinforcement Learning

CartPole Goes Brrr

One cartpole rig, nine increasingly ridiculous jobs. It starts as the textbook balance problem and ends as a stick figure walking from human motion capture. The point was to learn RL properly — which mostly meant learning how many ways a reward function can lie to you.

Three times the reward function lied

The through-line of this project is not the policies. It is that a reward function will always find the cheapest thing that satisfies it, and that "cheapest" is almost never what you meant. Each of these took real debugging to find.

1. The success rate that was never real

It wasn't 30%. It was 0%.

The two-link balance task reported 30% success for a long time. It was actually zero. The termination check tested an angle the pole physically could not reach — link1's collision geometry caught it on the floor first — so a face-plant simply ran out the clock and got scored as a win.

Three more bugs sat underneath: penalties linear in raw Newtons that made using the actuator net-negative (the agent settled on 6.6 N of a 100 N budget — it had learned that moving was a mistake), an un-normalised action space, and 240 Hz control that left gamma=0.99 with just 0.42 s of effective lookahead.

Rebuilt with geometric tip-height termination, a normalised action space, 60 Hz control, and nine reward terms collapsed into one — it now runs at a genuine 63%. Of the 37 failures in 100 episodes, 36 are the cart running out of rail with the pole still upright.

The crawl task — rail removed, the pole drags the cart forward like an inchworm using its own joint torques. Asymmetric friction (cart glides, tip grips) is the physical half of the trick.

2. "Moved forward" is not "walked"

The planar biped reached 6.4 m per episode against a random baseline of −0.02 m. It looked like progress. It wasn't walking — it was skating. Both feet stayed on the ground 98% of the time, the knees never left their straight hard stop, and there was more foot slip than actual forward travel.

Forward velocity paid exactly the same whether the robot stepped or slid, and nothing in the reward objected. Same shape of hole as the unreachable termination check: a metric measuring something adjacent to the thing you wanted.

3. PPO destroying a working policy

The imitation run reached near-full-episode tracking at 1.1M steps, then collapsed. approx_kl hit 100× its healthy range as the policy sharpened, and ten unguarded epochs per batch kept overshooting. The final model evaluated at 0% — while its own 1.1M checkpoint evaluated at 80%. Run 2 added target_kl, learning-rate decay, and dropped reward normalisation (the mimic reward is already bounded). It finished at 99% with no collapse.

The fix that replaced seven reward terms

The walking reward lineage — slip penalties, clearance bonuses, step machines, alternation discounts, overtake rules — was seven terms of hand-crafted economics, and what it bought was skating, then shuffling, then a gallop.

The final task throws all of it away for one idea from DeepMimic: a reference motion says where every joint should be at every phase of the gait, and the reward is simply closeness to it. "Walk like this" instead of "move forward without doing any of the nine things we thought of".

The reference is CMU motion capture of a human walking, retargeted through forward kinematics, sagittal projection, the URDF's joint conventions, Froude time scaling for legs 0.8× human length, and a root height solved on the robot's own foot geometry so the reference never floats or clips the floor. The body needed rebuilding for it too — the ankle moved to a third of the way along a longer foot (a centred foot has no toe-off lever) and mass ratios corrected to human proportions (v1 carried 16% of its mass in the trunk and head; a human carries about 45%).

The mimic pipeline end to end — CMU's capture, the human skeleton, the retargeted reference, and the policy running in real physics, phase-locked.

The part that matters: none of those sub-behaviours are named in the reward. Step alternation, 4.3 cm swing clearance, 85% single support, the collapse in foot slip — all of it falls out of "match the reference", and all of it had previously resisted seven hand-written terms trying to buy it directly.

Constraint as curriculum

Two tasks use a gantry — passive, frictionless prismatic rails that lock out rotation and sideways drift. This is the same approach real hopping research used (the Raibert hopper, the MIT Leg Lab): a boom that constrains the problem to 2D so you can study hopping dynamics without simultaneously solving 3D balance.

The rails never push, brake, or script the cart's trajectory — it moves purely from foot-ground contact forces. The agent still has to learn a genuine compress–extend–flight–land cycle. It just doesn't have to learn not to fall sideways at the same time. A constraint that removes a sub-problem is a curriculum tool, not a cheat — as long as you say which one it is.

The gantry-constrained hopper — a genuine compress, extend, flight, land cycle at 16.9 m per episode. The rails prevent tipping; they never provide propulsion.

What this project actually taught

  • Diagnostics before hyperparameters. Every real gain here came from finding a broken measurement, not from a better learning rate.
  • One geometric quantity beats nine hand-weighted terms — tip height replaced a per-joint cosine reward that was happily paying for a folded pose.
  • Some behaviours cannot be specified in reward terms, only demonstrated.
  • PPO can destroy a working policy as efficiently as it builds one if nothing bounds the update size.
  • If your success metric can be satisfied by a face-plant, you do not have a success metric.
Why this is on my portfolio

Most RL write-ups show the run that worked. This one keeps the post-mortems in the repository — the 0% that was reported as 30%, the skating that was reported as walking, the training collapse — because the ability to distrust your own results is the part of the job that transfers to real robots. On hardware, a metric that lies costs more than a policy that underperforms.
